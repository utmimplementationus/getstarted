Operational Prioritization

When two flights would conflict, which one yields? The cohort adopted two priority levels so that time-critical, life-safety missions aren't blocked by routine commercial flights.

This came out of a task force with the FAA, NASA, and industry. They recommended starting simple (two levels) and collecting data to validate how prioritization affects the airspace before adding complexity.

High Priority

Life-safety, time-critical

Reserved for missions where immediate response is essential for human safety:

  • Responding to a 911 call where safety of life is at stake
  • Active search-and-rescue
  • Delivering blood, plasma, AEDs, or Narcan when life is at stake
  • Disaster response where safety of life is at stake
  • Drone-as-first-responder (DFR) missions
Standard Priority

Everything else

All routine operations that don't meet the life-dependent bar:

  • Standard package delivery
  • Infrastructure inspection
  • Photography and data collection
  • Training missions (even for public-safety operators)
  • Routine delivery of basic medical supplies
How it behaves in the air. When a high-priority operation overlaps another, providers notify the affected operator per the ASTM standard, and accepted, standard priority flights must re-plan. Currently, no one is required to re-plan if in-flight. And at every level, two equal-priority flights are never allowed to conflict.

Qualifying to fly high priority

An operator can't just self-declare. To use high priority, you:

  • Run operational practicality simulations that realistically represent your mix of high- and standard-priority missions
  • Submit an issue ticket to the Operations Committee with evidence, such as an SGI waiver, a Certificate of Waiver or Authorization (COA), or a DFR Certificate of Waiver
  • For medical transport: a signed letter from a public/health entity engaging you, plus your attestation that you hold the required authorizations

No standard document fits? You can propose an alternative artifact with a rationale for the Operations Committee to review. Once your ticket is accepted, you may use high priority for all applicable missions.

DSS Pooling & Governance

The Discovery & Synchronization Service (DSS) is the shared backbone that lets providers find and sync with each other's operations. This policy keeps it robust and makes clear who owns what.

Predictable, yet flexible

Give providers and regulators predictability around the DSS pool, while allowing the flexibility to use other instances to keep the network robust.

Clear ownership

Every DSS instance is tied to a provider who keeps technical and governance responsibility for its performance.

Monitored over time

Pool performance is watched continuously, with governance and technical requirements adjusted to address safety or performance issues.

The essentials

  • There's one DSS pool for the US, matching the national boundaries.
  • Every instance in the pool must pass automated testing, proving it interoperates with all other instances and providers.
  • An instance must be provided by a signatory provider, or a subcontractor to one. If you subcontract, you're responsible for that instance meeting privacy and security requirements.
  • Each provider is expected to use its own DSS instance as much as practicable, and may use another pool instance only while its own is down.
  • Providers must be able to detect an outage, switch to another instance, detect recovery, and switch back.
  • Monthly performance reports are required, using the approved template.
Severity. A failure of a whole DSS instance is treated as a Severity 2 event. A single node failing within an instance does not, by itself, trigger SLA resolution.

Policies can change, by consensus

Any affected stakeholder can propose a change through the Issue Resolution process in the governance agreement (Annex C, Section 7(h)), including new priority levels or changes to who qualifies. That's the cohort's commitment to evolving with real operational learnings.
